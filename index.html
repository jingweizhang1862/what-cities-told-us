C1 · Methodological foundation

How do we know whether a policy actually worked?

Outcomes show up slowly and partially, and effects spill over to nearby streets.

In the paper: How to rigorously quantify the impacts of urban infrastructure and policy interventions?

From our interviews

8 stories

Transport

Car trip data pointed buses the wrong way

A ride-hailing platform that also ran buses first planned its bus routes around where its car trips started. It didn’t work, because people who take cars and people who take buses are different groups.

Tradeoff: the data you have vs. the data you need

Also in C2 ›

What happened next

Car trip data pointed buses the wrong way

The company guessed each user’s home and workplace from trip times (morning trips start at home, evening trips at work) and checked the guesses against saved addresses. That showed where people commute. It ignored heavy car users, who would never switch. Local staff picked every stop by hand. Riders cared more about frequent, on-time buses than exact routes, so buses stayed on main roads.

Transport

Only the complaints got fixed

A city repaired bike lanes when residents complained through 311, its non-emergency service line. Residents most familiar with city services, often in higher-income neighborhoods, complained far more than others. So problems in other neighborhoods stayed invisible.

Tradeoff: cheap complaint-based repairs vs. costly regular inspections

What happened next

Only the complaints got fixed

About two years before our interview, the city began regular inspections instead of waiting for complaints. Staff now ride every bike lane once a month and record problems themselves. When planning new projects, the city also tracks how much it invests in neighborhoods it has officially marked as underserved.

Public space

A survey that counts answers, not who answers

Before the city funds improvements to a shopping street, a local business group has to survey merchants and shoppers. The city only checks the number of responses. Nobody checks whether lower-income residents and other often-overlooked groups are among those who answered.

Tradeoff: an easy rule vs. a representative sample

Also in C3 ›

What happened next

A survey that counts answers, not who answers

Not solved yet. The city official we interviewed called this a gap: the city sets a minimum number of responses but has no way to check who answered.

Transport

Bike lanes judged only by ridership

After new downtown bike lanes opened, ridership jumped and the city publicized it widely. Ridership then became the main way people judge any bike lane. Suburban lanes will never reach downtown numbers, so suburban residents ask why they should get lanes at all.

Tradeoff: downtown numbers vs. suburban needs

What happened next

Bike lanes judged only by ridership

Not solved yet. One idea from the person we interviewed: also judge a lane by what it connects, such as access to jobs, food, and parks.

Transport

Five years to know if a street got safer

Bike crashes are rare, so it takes about five years of crash data to tell whether a new bike lane made a street safer. But most evaluations only look at the first year after a lane opens.

Tradeoff: quick answers vs. real evidence

What happened next

Five years to know if a street got safer

Not solved yet. There is no standard for when to evaluate a new lane: after six months, two years, or five? After one year, the city can measure how many people ride and what the public thinks of the lane. The person we interviewed would like research on when and how to run before-and-after studies.

Transport

Benefits promised, rarely checked

To win national funding, a transit expansion project must show its expected benefits: how many women and low-income riders it will serve, what jobs it may create, and its climate impact. Checking whether those benefits actually appear after it opens has only come up in the last five years or so.

Tradeoff: projected benefits vs. measured benefits

What happened next

Benefits promised, rarely checked

Checking is starting. Government officials have begun defining performance measures for projects to report after they open. When no good measure exists, as with emissions avoided, agencies report the best they can.

Public space

Safety measured by counting shoppers

A city agency that funds programs on shopping streets judges success by foot traffic, credit and debit card spending, and empty storefronts. It doesn’t measure safety directly, because officials see safety as too subjective.

Tradeoff: what’s easy to count vs. what matters

Also in C9 ›

What happened next

Safety measured by counting shoppers

The agency works on safety indirectly. Its grants pay for lighting, cleaning, and art or events that bring people out, on the idea that busy, clean, well-lit streets feel safer. Whether people actually feel safe is still not measured.

Transport

The app’s recommendations bury the shared ride

In a ride-hailing app, a recommendation model picks which ride options to show you, and it is tuned to get you to book right away. Cars arriving in two or three minutes do well. A cheaper shared ride that shows a 12-minute wait and a 5-minute walk does worse, even with its discount, so the system struggles to give it a place.

Tradeoff: bookings today vs. value over time

Also in C2 ›

What happened next

The app’s recommendations bury the shared ride

No clean fix. The team of the person we interviewed pushed for the company’s models to also count long-term value, such as repeat use, and argued for taking a short-term hit for a long-term bet. It was a constant internal battle, and hard to win because long-term value had no good measure.

For discussion

  • How do you separate a real effect from traffic that moved one street over?
  • What can you measure in year one that predicts year five?

Transport

The paving schedule decides the bike lane

A city picks which streets to rebuild based on how worn the pavement is, not on cycling needs. A raised, protected bike lane requires moving the storm drains, which only happens in a full rebuild. If a street is only due for a new surface, the bike lane ends up as painted lines.

Tradeoff: the paving schedule vs. the safest design

What happened next

The paving schedule decides the bike lane

Not solved yet. The person we interviewed said what is missing is joined-up planning: road budgets that also count cycling and safety benefits. Then a street due only for a new surface could be upgraded to a full rebuild.

For discussion

  • When is a simple model better than a detailed one?
  • How would you test a model on real streets before scaling it?

C4 · Housing Services · SDG 11.1 · Coordinate

How do we build housing allocation tools that caseworkers will use?

Caseworkers are the social workers who manage each client’s case. Assessments are self-reported and incomplete, and both people and available units keep changing.

In the paper: How to translate data-driven housing allocation policies into implementable decision-support systems?

From our interviews

1 story

Housing

Ranked by need, not by what will work

Housing for people experiencing homelessness is assigned through a mix of eligibility checks, self-reported vulnerability assessments, waiting lists, and caseworker judgment. Many of these prioritization tools were designed for other purposes and are not directly linked to how people fare after they are housed. And people waiting for housing move around, so they can be hard to reach when a unit opens.

Tradeoff: ranking by need vs. matching what will work

What happened next

Ranked by need, not by what will work

Caseworkers, the social workers who manage each case, know things the data rarely captures, such as clients’ housing needs and preferences. Cities are now working with researchers on tools that use predicted outcomes, like staying housed, when matching people to housing. Not solved yet: how to bring client preferences and caseworker knowledge into those tools.

For discussion

  • How can a model use what caseworkers know?
  • What should the tool optimize: speed, staying housed, or fairness?

C5 · Transportation · SDG 11.2 · Access

How do we bring good transit to fast-growing cities and underserved communities?

Data is unreliable, budgets are small, and needs like personal safety rarely show up in the data.

In the paper: How can the reach of sustainable transportation be expanded in developing countries and among vulnerable groups?

From our interviews

4 stories

Transport

A cheap way to test bus routes backfired

A platform wanted to test new bus routes without renting buses. It listed bus timetables in its app, but no bus actually ran. If someone booked, it sent a shared car instead and called it an upgrade. Many riders, especially women, cancelled these rides. A crowded bus feels public. A small car with two strangers can feel unsafe.

Tradeoff: cheap, flexible supply vs. feeling safe

What happened next

A cheap way to test bus routes backfired

No fix was found. The lesson: a cheaper substitute that looks like an upgrade on paper can feel like a downgrade to riders.

Transport

A premium bus stuck in ordinary traffic

A platform ran app-booked buses with air conditioning and guaranteed seats in a fast-growing city. Riders who used them loved them, but the buses sat in the same traffic as everyone else, and riders still walked on broken sidewalks to unmarked street corners.

Tradeoff: the service you sell vs. streets you don’t control

What happened next

A premium bus stuck in ordinary traffic

The team got routes, schedules, and the app working well, then got stuck. What riders needed was bus lanes so buses move faster, real stops, and better sidewalks, which only governments can provide. The lesson the person we interviewed drew: the problems are political more than technical.

Transport

Why a shared ride is never half price

You might expect two riders sharing a car to each pay half. But sharing saves the platform only one cost: the driver’s time. Insurance is charged per trip and doesn’t shrink with a second rider, and detours to pick that rider up add cost. So even a perfect match is far from half price. Riders also felt more uneasy sharing a car with one stranger than a bus with fifteen.

Tradeoff: small savings vs. lost comfort

What happened next

Why a shared ride is never half price

No fix was found. Bigger discounts would need lower costs. Asking riders to wait a few minutes so the app could find better matches clashed with the habit of “press a button, get a car.”

Transport

The airport shuttle paid off, the city bus didn’t

One platform ran a large app-based bus network in a fast-growing megacity and an airport shuttle in a high-income city. The shuttle made far more money: a taxi to the airport cost several times the shuttle fare, and traffic was predictable. In the megacity the need was far greater, but riders compared fares with very cheap public buses, and traffic made schedules unreliable.

Tradeoff: where the need is vs. where the money is

Also in C8 ›

What happened next

The airport shuttle paid off, the city bus didn’t

The airport shuttle was simply the better business. The person we interviewed sees serving the higher-need city as a matter of politics and funding: that city needs bus lanes, real stops, and walkable streets, which a private operator cannot provide alone.

For discussion

  • If you can’t fix the street, what can you fix?
  • How do you plan for safety when it is mostly a feeling?

C6 · Transportation · SDG 11.2 · Supply

How do we design robotaxis, delivery robots, and drones that improve access and safety?

Autonomous fleets still need chargers, curb space, and people behind them.

In the paper: How to design and operate autonomous transportation systems to improve access, equity, and safety in urban services?

From our interviews

3 stories

Transport

Cheaper rides, more traffic

If robotaxis (self-driving taxis) make rides very cheap, people will take many more rides. One practitioner compared this to text messages: people sent far more once each one stopped costing 50 cents. The cars may be electric, but the result could be “carbon-free congestion”: no exhaust, but more traffic jams.

Tradeoff: lower prices vs. more congestion

What happened next

Cheaper rides, more traffic

Not solved yet. The practitioner’s view: cities need rules that steer robotaxis toward shared rides and toward connecting people with public transit. Congestion pricing, a charge for driving into busy areas, is a start.

Transport

No driver, no one in charge

Fixed-route buses may be the easiest place to start with self-driving vehicles, since routes and times are set. But bus drivers also keep order on board. Without a driver, who steps in when a conflict breaks out on board?

Tradeoff: easier automation vs. no one in charge

What happened next

No driver, no one in charge

Not solved yet. The person we interviewed counts this among the real challenges for driverless public transit.

Resilience

Footbridges too low for fire trucks

Many footbridges over city roads are too low for the largest fire trucks to pass under, and those are the trucks that carry the equipment for high-rise fires. So for some fires high up in tall towers, the right equipment cannot reach the building at all.

Tradeoff: everyday street design vs. emergency access

Also in C7 ›

What happened next

Footbridges too low for fire trucks

Not solved yet. The options the person we interviewed mentioned were extreme, like taking the equipment or a bridge apart. Practitioners would rather see research on new equipment, such as drones for high-rise fires.

For discussion

  • What should people still do in an autonomous system?
  • Should cities price or cap robotaxi fleets?

C7 · Urban Resilience · SDG 11.5 · Supply

How should a city split its disaster budget between big infrastructure and local fixes?

Big infrastructure is expensive and slow to build. Local fixes are cheap but small.

In the paper: How to allocate limited budgets between major infrastructure and localized mitigation tools for disaster risks?

From our interviews

5 stories

Resilience

Footbridges too low for fire trucks

Many footbridges over city roads are too low for the largest fire trucks to pass under, and those are the trucks that carry the equipment for high-rise fires. So for some fires high up in tall towers, the right equipment cannot reach the building at all.

Tradeoff: everyday street design vs. emergency access

Also in C6 ›

What happened next

Footbridges too low for fire trucks

Not solved yet. The options the person we interviewed mentioned were extreme, like taking the equipment or a bridge apart. Practitioners would rather see research on new equipment, such as drones for high-rise fires.

Resilience

A hotel kept the flood out

Upgrading a city’s drains takes years. During one major rainstorm, a hotel stayed dry because it had installed its own flood barriers at its entrances.

Tradeoff: slow citywide upgrades vs. quick local fixes

What happened next

A hotel kept the flood out

The city adopted the idea quickly. Building managers and security staff are now responsible for putting up flood barriers at their buildings. In old districts that won’t get new drains soon, the same barriers help protect older residents and children.

Resilience

Where to keep emergency supplies

No one knows exactly where a storm will hit, so supplies kept in central stockpiles may be far from where they are needed.

Tradeoff: central stockpiles vs. supplies close to people

What happened next

Where to keep emergency supplies

The city splits supplies by type. Heavy equipment like pump trucks and excavators stays with the government. Sandbags and food are kept by neighborhoods, building managers, and an online retailer’s local warehouses. These local holders can hand supplies out on their own.

Resilience

No one will pay to rebuild old drains

Old drains can’t handle extreme rain. Rebuilding them means tearing up streets for years and earns no money, so private investors stay away. A giant underground tunnel that stores storm water works, but costs too much for most cities.

Tradeoff: one big tunnel vs. many small fixes

What happened next

No one will pay to rebuild old drains

The person we interviewed suggested treating old districts as weak points and protecting them with evacuation plans and flood barriers at entrances. New districts can set aside green space to absorb water from the start. In this person’s view, there is no shortcut: fixing the drains takes decades.

Resilience

Preventing fires instead of fighting them

In some dense older neighborhoods, buildings are packed close together and the alleys are too narrow to widen, so a fire can spread from building to building. Traditional firefighting there is near its limit.

Tradeoff: fighting fires vs. preventing them

What happened next

Preventing fires instead of fighting them

The city went after a main cause of fires there: low-quality e-bikes. It managed e-bike charging stations and took action against unsafe products. The expert we interviewed has a rule of thumb: better management solves most problems, and technology covers the rest.

For discussion

  • How would you split a budget between big and small fixes?
  • Who should hold emergency supplies, and who decides when to use them?

C8 · Urban Space · SDG 11.7 · Access

How do we get service into neighborhoods that providers would rather skip?

Public goals have to line up with what private operators and local groups can afford.

In the paper: How to develop practical mechanisms and operational policies to ensure equitable access to urban services and facilities?

5 stories

Transport

Scooters required where fewer people ride

Rental scooter companies need a city permit to operate. Permits require them to keep a share of their scooters in lower-income zones and to offer discounts there. Those zones bring fewer rides and less revenue, and moving scooters there costs money.

Tradeoff: required coverage vs. efficiency

What happened next

Scooters required where fewer people ride

Some cities offer a trade: serve the lower-income zones, and you may run more scooters downtown. Others cancel some permit fees for companies that serve those zones.

Public space

Cleaner streets where property is worth more

On many shopping streets, property owners form a business improvement district: each pays a fee, and the money buys extra street cleaning and lighting. Areas with higher property values raise more money. Districts with less funding spend most of their budget on basic upkeep, with little left for anything extra.

Tradeoff: local funding vs. equal quality

What happened next

Cleaner streets where property is worth more

The city gives extra grants to small, low-budget districts and pays for lighting and art to draw people in. When grants go mostly to areas with more active local groups, the city looks for applicants elsewhere and pairs small groups with larger organizations.

Public space

Insurance before cleanup

Neighborhood groups can take over care of a neglected plaza. But first they must buy insurance, sign an agreement protecting the city from lawsuits, and sometimes hire a lawyer. Groups in the areas with the fewest resources are the least able to do this.

Tradeoff: the city’s liability vs. who can take part

What happened next

Insurance before cleanup

Not solved yet. The city official we interviewed asked for two things: tools that teach small groups how to manage a public space, and better coordination between city agencies, since groups now face each agency’s paperwork separately. The official said the city knows what would help but lacks the money and staff.

For discussion

  • What contract gets an operator to serve neighborhoods it would skip?
  • How should a city divide top-up grants?
